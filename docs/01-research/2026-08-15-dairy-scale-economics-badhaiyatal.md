# Dairy Scale Economics — Badhaiyatal, Bardiya

**Research date:** 2026-08-15  
**Location:** Badhaiyatal Rural Municipality, Bardiya, Lumbini Province, Nepal  
**Decision:** Compare 20, 40, 60, 100 and 150 total-animal dairy models without a fixed capital budget.

## Executive conclusion

- Do **not** optimize the project around a predetermined budget.
- Do **not** start at 20 total animals if the objective is a professionally managed scalable dairy business; fixed labour, management, chilling/milking and compliance costs are poorly absorbed at this scale.
- Recommended initial operating target: **40–60 total animals**, with infrastructure master-planned for **~100 total animals**.
- Preferred launch design: approximately **24–36 lactating animals at steady state**, plus dry cows, heifers and calves; alternatively procure a core group of quality in-milk/pregnant females and build replacements internally.
- Expand toward ~100 total animals only after proving (1) milk offtake, (2) fodder/silage supply, (3) reproductive performance, (4) animal health and (5) payment discipline from the buyer.
- Avoid jumping directly to 150 total animals unless there is a signed high-volume buyer arrangement, sufficient irrigated fodder land/contracted forage, trained management, backup power/water, and working-capital reserve.

## Evidence base

1. Nepal's government-set raw milk benchmark has been reported around **NPR 65/L for milk at 4% fat and 8% SNF**, while actual private-dairy payments can be lower. This makes fat/SNF, buyer choice and collection terms material to profitability.
2. DDC reports a national collection network through cooperatives and has a Nepalgunj milk supply scheme, so institutional offtake exists in the broader western market, but farm-level acceptance and price must be contracted/verified.
3. DDC announced modernization of milk collection for FY 2083/84 and invited producer cooperatives/private farms interested in regular trade to contact it, strengthening the case for formal digital milk-quality and payment records.
4. Nepal dairy studies consistently identify feed as the dominant cost; a national study found feed roughly 61% of production cost. Therefore own-fodder/silage capability is the main structural cost advantage.
5. A Chitwan silage supply-chain study reported fodder-maize productivity around 53.9 t/ha in one dataset; another Nepal-linked silage study reported ~22 t/ha. The wide spread is why farm land planning below uses conservative ranges rather than a single yield.
6. HF/crossbred cattle in Nepal can materially outperform local cattle, but realized output depends on heat stress, feed, genetics, fertility and health; Nepal sources report improved-breed output substantially above local breeds and commercial HF-cross expectations commonly around the mid-teens L/day or higher under good management.

## Model definition

The animal counts below are **total mature-system herd size**, not number of milking cows.

Base herd structure used for comparison:

- Lactating: 60%
- Dry cows: 10%
- Heifers/replacements: 15%
- Calves/young stock: 15%

This structure is deliberately more realistic than assuming every purchased animal gives milk 365 days/year.

These are planning assumptions, not vendor quotations.

## Why 20 animals is not preferred

A 20-head total herd produces only about 12 lactating animals under a realistic steady-state structure. Two workers, basic farm management, milking equipment, power/water systems, records, biosecurity and transport are spread over too little milk. It can work as an owner-operated family farm, but it is not the best scale for the intended professional commercial system.

## Why 40–60 animals is the preferred launch zone

At 40–60 animals:

- fixed overhead starts to dilute materially;
- milk volume (roughly 336–504 L/day in the base case) is large enough for serious buyer negotiation but still operationally controllable;
- a four-person-or-smaller core livestock team can operate the herd depending on mechanization;
- disease, fertility and feeding mistakes are costly but not catastrophic at the same absolute level as 100–150 animals;
- the farm can validate imported/high-grade genetics before scaling;
- silage and manure systems can be tested before committing to much larger infrastructure;
- expansion to 100 can largely use the same management system if utilities, lanes, shed layout and milking system are designed for expansion from day one.

## Why 100 animals is the first major scale target

A 100-total-animal herd (~60 lactating in the base case) produces approximately **840 L/day** at 14 L/lactating cow/day. This approaches the volume where:

- dedicated milk chilling becomes strategically useful;
- direct contracts with larger dairies become more attractive;
- feed procurement and silage production can justify dedicated machinery;
- manure becomes a meaningful biogas/vermicompost feedstock;
- specialist herd management and reproduction records have large financial value.

The 100-head model is recommended as a **Phase 2 target**, not necessarily Day-1 stocking.

## Why 150 animals should not be Day 1

At ~90 lactating animals, base production is ~1,260 L/day. That creates concentration risk:

- buyer must reliably absorb >1,000 L/day;
- delayed dairy payments create large working-capital exposure;
- one ration error affects a very large daily milk volume;
- fodder shortfalls force expensive bought feed;
- heat stress, mastitis and fertility management require professional systems;
- labour supervision and night coverage become much more important.

150 total animals can be attractive **after** the 100-head system is stable.

## Fodder / land planning

Feed should be treated as infrastructure, not as a monthly shopping expense.

Use a planning range of roughly **0.10–0.18 ha of productive irrigated fodder area per total animal-equivalent** if the objective is to supply a large portion of green fodder/silage internally. Actual requirement depends heavily on cropping intensity, Napier yield, maize silage yield, purchased concentrates, crop residues and irrigation.

Indicative planning envelope:

| Total herd | Intensive fodder-land planning range | Approx. Terai bigha (1 bigha ~0.677 ha) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 | 2–4 ha | 3–6 bigha |
| 40 | 4–7 ha | 6–10 bigha |
| 60 | 6–11 ha | 9–16 bigha |
| 100 | 10–18 ha | 15–27 bigha |
| 150 | 15–27 ha | 22–40 bigha |

This is a **planning envelope, not a legal/agronomic requirement**. Contract farming or purchased silage can reduce owned/leased acreage but raises price and supply risk.

## Breed strategy for Bardiya/Terai

Recommended commercial direction:

1. **HF-cross / high-grade Holstein cross** for volume, only where shade, fans/sprinklers, clean water and high-quality ration are assured.
2. **Jersey cross / HF×Jersey-type animals** as a resilience component where higher fat, heat tolerance and lower maintenance are valuable.
3. Avoid building the economics around exotic genetic potential (e.g., 30+ L/day). Underwrite the project at **12–14 L/day annual lactating average** and treat higher yield as upside.
4. Purchase based on verified lactation records, pregnancy status, udder/teat conformation, mastitis testing, age/parity, vaccination and disease screening—not seller claims.

## Suggested phased build

### Phase 1 — prove the system

**Target:** 40–60 total animals at stabilized herd composition.  
Build civil/utilities layout so it can become a 100-head farm without demolishing core structures.

Priority assets:

- expandable loose-housing shed with heat-stress control;
- quarantine/sick pen and calving pen;
- reliable water storage and troughs;
- milking machine sized for expansion;
- milk-quality testing (fat/SNF, adulteration controls, mastitis testing);
- small bulk milk cooler or secured nearby chilling arrangement depending buyer pickup;
- chaff cutter / TMR preparation appropriate to herd size;
- silage bunker/pits;
- manure channel, solids separation and planned biogas/vermicompost interface;
- digital animal ID and daily milk/breeding/health records;
- generator/solar/backup solution for water, cooling and milk handling.

### Phase 2 — scale to ~100 total animals

Trigger only when all of these are met for at least several consecutive months:

- buyer pays on time;
- average saleable yield ≥ target;
- conception/calving interval is on plan;
- mastitis and mortality within thresholds;
- ≥70% of planned roughage requirement secured in advance;
- trained supervisor and relief labour available;
- milk rejection/spoilage negligible;
- adequate working-capital reserve.

### Phase 3 — consider 150+

Proceed only with contracted milk offtake, professional herd manager, robust feed contracts/land base and validated value streams from manure/biogas/compost.

## Investment-envelope approach

Do not lock a budget before quotations and the land/fodder decision. For screening purposes, **excluding land purchase**, the likely capital requirement rises sharply with quality of animals, shed standard, cooling, mechanization and working capital. Obtain live quotations before using these values for financing.

The correct next financial model should itemize:

- animals by class and acquisition strategy;
- shed/civil works per adult-equivalent;
- milking + chilling;
- water/electricity/solar/backup;
- feed storage + silage;
- manure/biogas/vermicompost plant;
- machinery/tractor requirements;
- fodder establishment/irrigation;
- veterinary/quarantine setup;
- working capital (minimum 3–6 months);
- insurance;
- financing costs net of government interest subsidy;
- applicable FY 2083/84 grants only after official calls open.

## Critical due diligence before selecting the final size

1. Obtain **written milk purchase offers** from at least 3 buyers/collection routes in Bardiya/Nepalgunj region, with fat/SNF formula, rejection rules, transport responsibility and payment days.
2. Test soil and irrigation capacity of the actual farm/fodder plots.
3. Obtain live quotations for quality HF/Jersey cross in-milk and pregnant animals from traceable farms.
4. Obtain feed ingredient prices delivered to Badhaiyatal and formulate a ration with a livestock nutritionist.
5. Verify electricity reliability and summer heat-stress design requirements.
6. Confirm local veterinary/AI coverage and emergency response.
7. Confirm insurer valuation rules and current government premium subsidy.
8. Confirm available concessional lending and bank collateral structure.
