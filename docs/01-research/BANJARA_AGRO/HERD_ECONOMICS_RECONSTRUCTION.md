# Banjara Agro Herd Economics Reconstruction

**Status:** Research model — not audited accounts  
**Reviewed:** 2026-08-14  
**Purpose:** Reconstruct the likely herd, feed, labour, capital and milk economics of Banjara Agro from public evidence. This document separates reported facts from calculations and scenario assumptions.

---

## 1. Executive conclusion

Banjara Agro should not be evaluated as a simple `number of cows × litres × milk price` farm.

Public evidence shows an integrated operation containing land, high-value cattle, silage production, a feed/fodder business, mechanised feeding/cleaning/milking, cooling, milk chilling and processing, manure/biogas infrastructure, vegetable production, breeding/replacement activity and a significant workforce.

The public numbers are useful, but they do **not** form a clean set of audited accounts. Several reported figures conflict with one another. Therefore this reconstruction uses three categories:

- **Reported** — directly stated in a public source.
- **Derived** — arithmetic performed on reported data.
- **Scenario** — an explicit assumption used to understand a range of possible economics.

The most important findings are:

1. **Feed security is central.** The 2023 report of 800 tonnes of silage stocked from early July until the next Baisakh implies roughly 2.83 tonnes/day, about 19.5 kg/day for each of the 145 reported mother cows if those cows are the feeding base.
2. At the reported whole-maize purchase price of NPR 4/kg, the **raw maize crop alone** represents about NPR 11,300/day or **NPR 10.3 per litre of the 1,100 L/day milk sold**. This excludes chopping, transport, ensiling losses, concentrate, hay, minerals, labour, veterinary cost, utilities and capital cost.
3. Banjara is extremely capital intensive at its reported current output. The 2023 report of ~NPR 40 crore invested against 145 mother cows equals roughly **NPR 27.6 lakh of total project capital per reported mother cow**. This is not cow purchase cost; it includes land and integrated infrastructure.
4. The reported cow purchase value (~NPR 2.5 lakh/head) means 145 cows would represent only about **NPR 3.63 crore**, roughly 9% of the reported NPR 40 crore first-phase investment. The expensive part is the system around the cows.
5. Current output and reported individual cow-yield claims do not reconcile cleanly. At 1,100 L/day sold and a public individual-cow range of 17–33 L/day, only about 33–65 lactating cows would be required if all milk were sold. This means either a large share of the 145 adult cows was non-lactating, much milk was diverted to processing/calves/other use, the yield range represented selected cows rather than herd average, or the public figures describe different time snapshots.
6. Banjara's public revenue/income figures are internally inconsistent and should **not** be used as audited profitability numbers.
7. Their large investment only makes economic sense if asset utilisation rises substantially and revenue is diversified across milk, processed dairy, cattle/genetics, manure, fodder/silage and other farm activities.

## 3. Milk productivity reconstruction

### 3.1 Farm-level milk intensity

These are **not lactating-cow yields**. They simply divide reported farm output by reported animal counts.

| Snapshot | Reported animals | Reported milk | Derived milk per reported animal |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Jan 2022 | 50 cows | 600 L/day | 12.0 L/cow/day |
| Apr 2022 | 112 cows | >=1,200 L/day handled (1,000 sold + 200 processed) | >=10.7 L/cow/day |
| Jul 2023 | 145 mother cows | 1,100 L/day sold | 7.6 L/mother-cow/day sold |
| Feb 2025 | ~180 total cattle incl. calves | 1,300 L/day produced | 7.2 L/total-head/day |

These ratios fall as the herd grows because the denominator increasingly contains dry cows, pregnant animals, calves/heifers and possibly cows in different lactation stages. They should never be interpreted as actual lactating-cow yield.

### 3.2 Reconstructing the likely lactating group in 2023

The 2023 source says cows on the farm were giving **17–33 L/day**, while the farm sold **1,100 L/day**.

If 1,100 L/day were the entire production:

| Average milk/lactating cow | Lactating cows needed | % of 145 mother cows |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 17 L | 65 | 44.6% |
| 20 L | 55 | 37.9% |
| 22 L | 50 | 34.5% |
| 25 L | 44 | 30.3% |
| 27 L | 41 | 28.1% |
| 30 L | 37 | 25.3% |
| 33 L | 33 | 23.0% |

This cannot be used to say “Banjara only had 50 milking cows.” It shows that the public numbers are incomplete.

Possible explanations:

- 1,100 L was **milk sold**, not total milk produced.
- Milk may have been used for calves, curd, paneer or other products.
- The 17–33 L range may describe selected/current milkers rather than herd average.
- 145 “mother cows” may include many dry and pregnant cows.
- Figures may have been collected at different dates.

### 3.3 Commercial benchmark for dry-cow share

Virginia Tech Extension notes that a balanced mature dairy-cow herd commonly has around **14–16% dry cows**. This benchmark applies to mature cows, not total cattle including calves/heifers.

Source: Virginia Cooperative Extension.  
https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/content/pubs_ext_vt_edu/en/DASC/DASC-51/DASC-51.html

This reinforces the need for our own future farm to track separate counts for:

- lactating cows,
- far-off dry cows,
- close-up dry cows,
- pregnant heifers,
- growing heifers,
- calves,
- bulls/other animals.

A single “total cows” number is not enough for economics.

## 4. Silage reconstruction

### 4.1 Reported 2023 feed facts

Kantipur reported:

- 800 tonnes of silage stored,
- stock intended to last until the next Baisakh,
- report date: 5 July 2023,
- 145 mother cows,
- 30 bigha of contracted maize,
- 20 participating farmers,
- whole maize plant purchase price: NPR 4/kg.

Taking Baisakh 1, 2081 as approximately 13 April 2024 gives about **283 days**.

### 4.2 Implied daily silage use

`800,000 kg / 283 days = ~2,827 kg/day`

If divided across the 145 reported mother cows:

`2,827 / 145 = ~19.5 kg/cow/day as-fed`

This is a reasonable **derived planning estimate**, but not a measured ration because calves/heifers, wastage and inventory safety stock are unknown.

### 4.3 Implied annual silage requirement

At the same usage rate:

`2.827 tonnes/day × 365 = ~1,032 tonnes/year`

So Banjara's 145-mother-cow system appears to need on the order of **1,000 tonnes/year of silage** at that feeding intensity, before accounting for herd expansion.

### 4.4 Silage requirement per 100 reported mother cows

`1,032 t/year × 100/145 ≈ 712 t/year per 100 mother cows`

Use this only as a Banjara-derived benchmark, not a universal nutritional prescription.

### 4.5 Raw maize crop cost

At NPR 4/kg:

`2,827 kg/day × NPR 4 = ~NPR 11,307/day`

Annual raw whole-maize purchase equivalent:

`NPR 11,307 × 365 ≈ NPR 4.13 million/year`

Against 1,100 L/day milk sold:

`NPR 11,307 / 1,100 L = ~NPR 10.3/L`

This **NPR 10.3/L is only the raw whole-maize crop component**. It is not total feed cost and not total milk production cost.

Missing items include:

- field harvest/chopping,
- transport,
- baling/packing or bunker work,
- fermentation/storage loss,
- feed-out loss,
- concentrate,
- grains/protein,
- hay/straw,
- green fodder,
- minerals/salt/additives,
- feeding labour and machinery,
- water and power.

### 4.6 Land intensity signal

In 2023, 30 bigha of contract maize was reported and the same article says farmers can grow two maize cycles annually. If the farm truly needs around 1,032 tonnes/year, then the economic question is not simply “how many bigha?” but:

- harvested tonnes per bigha per crop,
- crops/year,
- dry matter percentage,
- ensiling losses,
- delivered cost/kg,
- quality consistency.

For our project, the correct KPI should become **NPR per kg of usable silage dry matter**, not only land area.

## 5. Labour reconstruction

### Public workforce numbers

| Year | Reported animals | Reported jobs | Jobs per 100 reported animals |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2022 Apr | 112 cows | 30 | 26.8 |
| 2023 Jul | 145 mother cows | 42 | 29.0 |
| 2025 Feb | ~180 cattle incl. calves | >50 | >27.8 |

This does **not** mean a dairy needs 28 workers per 100 cows.

Banjara's workforce appears to cover more than basic cow care, including dairy processing, fodder/silage, vegetable operations, management, technical work and possibly delivery/logistics. The 2023 workforce also included 10 daily-wage workers.

The important lesson is that automation did not make the total enterprise labour-free. It changed labour from only manual cow work toward a broader integrated operating team.

For our farm we should separately budget:

1. cow care / feeding,
2. milking,
3. calf/heifer care,
4. fodder/silage,
5. cleaning/manure,
6. veterinary/reproduction,
7. administration/security,
8. milk delivery/processing if applicable.

## 6. Capital reconstruction

### 6.1 2023 reported capital per cow

Reported first-phase investment:

`NPR 40 crore`

Reported mother cows:

`145`

Derived total project capital per mother cow:

`40 crore / 145 = ~NPR 27.6 lakh per mother cow`

This number is intentionally shocking because it demonstrates that Banjara is **not mainly an animal-purchase project**.

### 6.2 What share was actually cattle?

Reported cow value:

`NPR 2.5 lakh/head`

For 145 cows:

`145 × 2.5 lakh = NPR 3.625 crore`

As share of NPR 40 crore:

`3.625 / 40 = ~9.1%`

So, using comparable public figures, only about **9% of total reported project investment** is represented by the purchase value of the 145 cows.

### 6.3 Land

The 2022 report states five bigha land cost about NPR 11 crore.

Relative to a NPR 40 crore project:

`11 / 40 = 27.5%`

### 6.4 Residual system capital

If we subtract reported land and nominal cow purchase value from NPR 40 crore:

`40 - 11 - 3.625 = NPR 25.375 crore`

This residual is not an audited “equipment cost.” It may include:

- sheds,
- milking parlour,
- processing/chilling,
- feed factory,
- silage infrastructure,
- cooling,
- manure/biogas,
- utilities,
- vehicles,
- greenhouses/vegetable infrastructure,
- buildings,
- working-capital items,
- other project assets.

But it demonstrates where the majority of capital went: **the production system, not the cows**.

### 6.5 Expansion shed economics

In 2023 management said a shed for **88 additional cows** would require around **NPR 4 crore**.

Derived shed capital:

`4 crore / 88 = ~NPR 4.55 lakh per additional cow place`

If those 88 cows also had to be purchased at NPR 2.5 lakh/head:

- cows: ~NPR 2.2 crore,
- shed: ~NPR 4.0 crore,
- minimum combined: ~NPR 6.2 crore,
- before feed inventory, working capital and other expansion equipment.

This is a major lesson for our farm: **capacity should be designed for expansion, but expensive capacity should not be built years before it can be utilised unless financing is exceptionally cheap.**

## 7. Asset-utilisation problem

Banjara originally targeted 5,000 L/day. Current reported milk volume in 2023 was 1,100 L/day sold.

If we very crudely treat the reported NPR 40 crore as containing NPR 11 crore land, then about NPR 29 crore is non-land capital. Not all of this belongs to dairy milk, and assets have different useful lives, so this is only a sensitivity test.

If NPR 29 crore were depreciated uniformly:

| Assumed life | Depreciation equivalent at 1,100 L/day | At 5,000 L/day target |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 years | ~NPR 72/L | ~NPR 15.9/L |
| 15 years | ~NPR 48/L | ~NPR 10.6/L |
| 20 years | ~NPR 36/L | ~NPR 7.9/L |

This explains Banjara's economic logic better than “high-tech machines save labour.”

The system was built for **much higher throughput and multiple revenue streams**. At low utilisation, capital cost per litre is enormous. At 5,000 L/day, the same fixed infrastructure becomes much more efficient.

Therefore our COW Farm should follow the opposite capital discipline:

> Build the site and layout for 200+ animals, but only install expensive equipment when herd size and milk throughput can utilise it.

## 8. Revenue reconstruction and contradictions

### 8.1 2022 inconsistency

Public 2022 figures say:

- 1,000 L/day milk sold,
- milk price NPR 70/L,
- 200 L/day used for curd/paneer,
- monthly income NPR 15 lakh.

But raw milk sales alone would mathematically be:

`1,000 × 70 × 30 = NPR 21 lakh/month`

before curd/paneer revenue.

Therefore “NPR 15 lakh monthly income” cannot simultaneously mean gross sales under the same assumptions. It could mean net income, a different period, only one business unit, or imprecise media reporting.

### 8.2 2023 inconsistency

The 2023 report says:

- 1,100 L/day milk sold,
- around NPR 5 crore annual sales from milk + manure.

Annual milk sold:

`1,100 × 365 = 401,500 L`

If NPR 5 crore were divided across those litres, the equivalent would be:

`NPR 50,000,000 / 401,500 = ~NPR 124.5/L`

This does **not** mean Banjara received NPR 124.5/L for raw milk. The figure includes manure and possibly other milk/value-added revenue, and public reporting may use turnover figures from a wider business scope.

### 8.3 2025 inconsistency

The 2025 report again says roughly NPR 15 lakh/month “income” while output is around 1,300 L/day and the farm also produces dairy products.

Even at the old NPR 70/L reference price:

`1,300 × 70 × 30 = NPR 27.3 lakh/month`

So the 15-lakh figure again cannot be interpreted safely as gross milk sales.

### Conclusion on revenue

Do **not** use the NPR 15 lakh/month or NPR 5 crore/year numbers as audited profit.

For our project, we will maintain separate accounting lines for:

- litres produced,
- litres sold raw,
- litres processed,
- raw milk sales,
- dairy product sales,
- cattle/heifer sales,
- manure/compost/biogas value,
- fodder/silage sales,
- grants,
- other revenue,
- cash operating cost,
- depreciation,
- interest,
- principal repayment,
- owner capital expenditure.

## 9. Cost-per-litre reconstruction

### 9.1 What we can calculate directly

Banjara-specific 2023 raw whole-maize component:

**~NPR 10.3/L of milk sold**

This is a high-confidence arithmetic derivation from the reported silage inventory, time coverage, maize price and milk sold.

### 9.2 Nepal dairy benchmarks

A 2022 Agriculture Development Journal study of 240 dairy farms in Bharatpur, Chitwan found average **variable cost of cow milk production around NPR 54.80/L**.

Source:  
https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/adj/article/view/51619

A 2026 peer-reviewed milk value-chain study using 2023 Kathmandu Valley farm data found an average **total milk production cost around NPR 74.25/L**, with feed the largest cost followed by labour. The same paper cites a National Dairy Development Board range of roughly NPR 45–67/L for earlier national studies.

Source:  
https://arccjournals.com/journal/asian-journal-of-dairy-and-food-research/DR-2309

A Nepal Agricultural Research Council/National Cattle Research Program summary states that quality-forage systems can reduce concentrate use by 35–45%, demonstrating why Banjara's investment in silage and fodder is strategically important. Absolute old rupee figures from that research should not be compared directly with 2026 prices.

Source:  
https://ncrp.gov.np/content/78/2019/77643637/

### 9.3 Banjara working cost scenarios — NOT reported facts

Because Banjara's actual concentrate use, payroll, utilities, vet cost, losses and debt terms are unavailable, use a sensitivity model rather than one fake precise number.

| Cash operating cost scenario | NPR/L | Annual OPEX at 1,100 L/day |
|---|---:|---:|
| Highly efficient large-farm case | 45 | ~NPR 1.81 crore |
| Near 2022 Chitwan cow-farm benchmark | 55 | ~NPR 2.21 crore |
| Higher-cost case | 65 | ~NPR 2.61 crore |

These scenarios are cash operating-cost sensitivities only. They are **not** Banjara's audited cost.

### 9.4 Full economic cost can be far higher at low asset utilisation

At current output, adding even a rough capital depreciation allocation makes full cost much higher. That is why we should not judge a large integrated project on raw milk margin alone.

A farm with heavy automation must either:

- run high throughput,
- capture premium milk pricing,
- process milk into higher-value products,
- sell genetics/cattle,
- monetise manure/fodder,
- receive grants or cheap capital,
- or combine several of these.

Banjara appears to be pursuing multiple parts of this strategy.

## 10. Replacement herd and breeding economics

By 2025 management reported that Banjara was producing its own cattle and selling some, reducing dependence on purchases from India.

This is economically important because purchased cows were publicly valued around NPR 2.5 lakh/head.

However, public sources do **not** provide:

- annual calvings,
- conception rate,
- pregnancy rate,
- services per conception,
- abortion/stillbirth rate,
- calf mortality,
- heifer mortality,
- age at first calving,
- calving interval,
- annual cull rate,
- replacement heifers entering the herd,
- sexed-semen conception rate,
- heifers/cows sold annually.

Therefore no honest Banjara replacement-rate calculation can yet be made.

For benchmarking, Penn State Extension shows that a 100-cow herd with a 30% replacement/cull rate, 24-month first calving and 10% heifer non-completion needs about **66 heifers in the replacement pipeline** to maintain herd size. This is a planning benchmark, not a Banjara fact.

Source:  
https://extension.psu.edu/heifer-economics

The University of Maryland Extension also gives a formal replacement-supply framework based on herd size, calving interval, heifer percentage, mortality and age at first calving.

Source:  
https://extension.umd.edu/resource/managing-heifer-inventory-dairy

For our farm, breeding must be modelled as a **two-year inventory pipeline**, not simply “half the calves are female.”

## 11. What Banjara appears to do well economically

### Feed integration

Banjara converted feed risk into a structured supply system through contract maize, advance financing to growers, silage inventory and Kunti Cattle Fodder.

Official Banjara Group description says Kunti Cattle Fodder focuses on corn silage and concentrate feed and aims to grow corn for quality and competitive pricing.

Source:  
https://www.banjaraagro.com/kunti-cattle-fodder

### Genetic self-replacement

The project moved from difficulty sourcing improved cows to producing cattle internally and selling some. This reduces import/purchase dependence and can create a second revenue stream.

### Mechanisation of bottlenecks

Milking, manure handling, feed preparation, chilling and cooling are mechanised. These systems improve consistency and can allow higher herd scale.

### Integration beyond raw milk

Milk processing, manure/biogas, fodder, cattle sales and vegetables diversify revenue and improve use of fixed assets.

### Building for future throughput

The 5,000 L/day target makes the large capital base more understandable. Banjara's economics are highly sensitive to utilisation.

## 12. What we should NOT copy blindly

1. **Do not copy NPR 40 crore capital intensity.**
2. **Do not build processing capacity before milk volume exists.**
3. **Do not buy automation because it looks modern.** Every machine must have a payback case.
4. **Do not mix farm businesses in one profit number.** Milk, cattle, fodder and vegetables need separate P&Ls.
5. **Do not use peak yield as herd yield.** Track litres per lactating cow and per total cow separately.
6. **Do not raise every female calf automatically.** Replacement inventory consumes feed and capital.
7. **Do not depend on purchased fodder without an annual feed-security plan.**
8. **Do not assume grants make an asset economical.** The asset must still earn a return after grant support.

## 13. Direct implications for our COW Farm

### Farm KPI dashboard required from day one

Every day/month we should record:

- total cattle,
- lactating cows,
- dry cows,
- pregnant cows,
- calves 0–3 months,
- calves 3–6 months,
- heifers 6–12 months,
- heifers 12–18 months,
- bred heifers,
- litres produced,
- litres saleable,
- litres sold,
- litres fed to calves,
- litres processed,
- milk/cow/day for lactating cows,
- concentrate kg/cow/day,
- silage kg/cow/day,
- feed cost/litre,
- labour cost/litre,
- veterinary/reproduction cost/litre,
- electricity/fuel cost/litre,
- total cash OPEX/litre,
- full cost/litre,
- conception rate,
- pregnancy rate,
- calving interval,
- calf mortality,
- heifer age at first calving,
- cull/death rate.

### Capital rule

Our project should be **layout-scalable but equipment-staged**.

Example philosophy:

- Phase 1: high-quality shed, drainage, feed alley, water, calf area, silage, basic milking system.
- Phase 2: mechanise repetitive labour bottlenecks.
- Phase 3: add chilling/processing only when milk throughput supports it.
- Phase 4: scale breeding/genetics and sell surplus replacement animals.

### Feed rule

Before purchasing cows, secure at least one complete annual feed plan with:

- tonnes of silage required,
- green fodder acreage,
- dry fodder inventory,
- concentrate formula and price,
- drought/monsoon buffer,
- storage-loss allowance,
- cost per kg usable dry matter.
