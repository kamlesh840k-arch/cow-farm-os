# Feed & Fodder Master Plan

## Objective

Make feed security the first production system, not an afterthought. The farm should be able to feed animals through heat, monsoon disruption and market-price spikes without emergency ration changes.

## Locked Phase-1 feed strategy — land first, hydroponics only if needed

**Decision date:** 2026-08-20

Cow Farm OS will follow this priority order for the initial 10-cow farm:

1. **Use available farm land first for green fodder/grasses.**
2. Grow the maximum practical quantity of suitable perennial and seasonal fodders before spending on hydroponic infrastructure.
3. **Purchase maize grain/feed ingredients, bran, and dry roughage** where buying them is more practical than using scarce land for those components.
4. Preserve or purchase silage when required to bridge seasonal shortages.
5. Measure actual daily green-fodder production versus herd requirement.
6. **Install hydroponic maize/wheat fodder only if land-grown green fodder plus stored/purchased forage is not adequate or reliable enough.**

Hydroponics is therefore classified as a **Phase-2 feed-security/shortfall module**, not the primary Phase-1 forage system.

### Why this order is preferred

- Farm-grown grass normally has lower cost per kg of usable dry matter than hydroponic sprouts.
- Existing land is a sunk/available productive resource and should be utilized first.
- Hydroponic cereal fodder is moisture-rich and should remain supplementary rather than replacing the basal forage ration.
- Delaying hydroponic CAPEX lets the farm first learn its real land yield, seasonal shortages, purchased-feed prices and cow consumption.
- If a genuine gap remains, the hydroponic system can then be sized to the **measured shortage**, avoiding overbuilding.

### Trigger for hydroponic investment

Do not build the full hydroponic unit merely because it is technically possible. Consider installation when one or more of the following are repeatedly observed:

- land-grown green fodder cannot meet daily requirement,
- dry-season/winter green-fodder shortage is material,
- purchased green fodder becomes unreliable or expensive,
- expansion in cow numbers creates a persistent green-feed deficit,
- land is better used for higher-value purposes and hydroponics becomes economically justified,
- an emergency/resilience green-fodder reserve is required.

Before investment, quantify the shortage in **kg fresh fodder/day and kg dry matter/day** and size the hydroponic unit only to cover that deficit.

## Phase-1 land target

For 30 adult breeding females plus youngstock:
- **4.5–5 bigha silage maize** under reliable irrigation and crop rotation.
- **3.5–4 bigha perennial green fodder** (e.g. suitable Napier/hybrid grass after local agronomic validation).
- **1–2 bigha flexible seasonal fodder/legume/oats/nursery/recovery area**.

Total planning target: **8–10 bigha productive irrigated fodder area**.

For 50 adult females plus youngstock: plan **15–18 bigha** unless contracted roughage/silage is intentionally used.

For the current 10-cow start, land allocation must be scaled to the **actual land available**, with priority given to high-yield perennial green fodder and complementary seasonal fodders. Do not delay the project solely because the longer-term 30-cow land target is not yet available.

## Steady-state feed requirement at 30 adults

Planning daily requirement including youngstock:
- silage: ~607 kg/day,
- green fodder: ~543 kg/day,
- straw/dry roughage: ~171 kg/day,
- concentrate: ~184 kg/day.

Annual approximate requirements:
- silage fed: ~221 t,
- green fodder fed: ~198 t,
- straw: ~62 t,
- concentrate: ~67 t.

Allow at least 15% loss between field harvest, storage and feed-out when sizing silage crop requirement.

## Silage system

1. Plant in staggered blocks to reduce harvest risk.
2. Harvest at correct whole-plant stage for fermentation.
3. Chop uniformly.
4. Pack rapidly and densely.
5. Exclude air immediately.
6. Design bunker width so daily face removal is adequate; do not build a huge bunker that leaves the face exposed for weeks.
7. Record each pit/bunker: crop, field, harvest date, estimated tonnes, opening date and spoilage.
8. Maintain 90-day feed inventory minimum before scaling herd.

## Feed testing

Quarterly or batch-based testing should include where available:
- dry matter,
- crude protein,
- fiber fractions,
- energy estimate,
- silage pH/fermentation quality,
- mycotoxin risk when suspected.

Bardiya's Integrated Agriculture & Livestock Development Office publishes feed-testing service availability in its citizen charter.

## Procurement policy

Concentrate, maize/other purchased grain, bran, mineral feed and dry roughage must be purchased against appropriate quality controls.

For manufactured/concentrate/mineral feed:
- batch ID,
- manufacturer,
- guaranteed analysis,
- manufacturing/expiry date,
- invoice,
- visual/smell check,
- sample retention for major batches where practical.

For grain/bran/dry roughage:
- supplier and invoice,
- delivered weight,
- moisture/visual condition,
- mold/insect check,
- foreign-material check,
- odor check,
- price per kg delivered,
- representative sample retention for large lots where practical.

## Feed-cost controls

Targets:
- feed <=70% of cash operating cost,
- ration change only after cost-per-litre analysis,
- track feed cost per lactating cow and per litre weekly,
- no concentrate increase unless incremental milk margin justifies it,
- no emergency green-fodder purchase as a normal operating practice.

### Land-first KPI

Track weekly:

- kg green fodder harvested from own land,
- kg green fodder required by herd,
- % of green-fodder demand supplied from own land,
- purchased green-fodder kg and cost,
- silage usage,
- dry roughage usage,
- concentrate/grain/bran usage,
- fodder deficit kg/day.

Hydroponic sizing, if eventually required, must be based on the measured persistent deficit rather than an assumed 50 kg/day target.

## Hydroponics status

**Status: DEFERRED / CONDITIONAL.**

Research and design already completed remain retained in Cow Farm OS for rapid deployment if needed. The system should be modular and cereal-flexible (maize/wheat), but no major hydroponic CAPEX should be committed until land-grown forage performance has been measured.

## Banjara lesson applied

Banjara's strongest transferable lesson is feed integration: secure maize/silage volume before herd expansion. Our version stays smaller, keeps fixed equipment light, prioritizes available land for grass/fodder, purchases selected feed components when sensible, and measures local crop yields before adding hydroponics or large TMR/mixing machinery.
