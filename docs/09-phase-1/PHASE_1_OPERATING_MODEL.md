# Phase 1 Operating Model — Banjara Intelligence Applied to COW Farm

**Status:** Working model, not a final investment decision  
**Model date:** 2026-08-14  
**Geography:** Bardiya / western Terai planning context  
**Land purchase:** Excluded from CAPEX  
**Currency:** NPR

## Executive recommendation

Do not copy Banjara Agro's capital intensity. Copy its sequencing:

1. genetics and replacement pipeline,
2. feed security and silage,
3. heat management and cow comfort,
4. clean mechanized milking,
5. chilling and milk-quality control,
6. manure/biogas/compost integration,
7. automate further only when throughput justifies it.

The recommended commercial Phase 1 is a **30-breeding-female foundation herd**, purchased at mixed stages rather than 30 cows all at peak milk:
- 24 mature lactating/pregnant cows,
- 6 in-calf heifers,
- site and utilities designed for 50 adult cows,
- youngstock/calving areas designed separately so herd growth does not overcrowd the milking shed.

This is deliberately not constrained to a 40–50 lakh budget. A properly engineered 30-female system is currently modeled at **~NPR 1.54 crore CAPEX, or ~NPR 1.74 crore including ~3 months working capital**, excluding land. These are allowances to be replaced with supplier quotations.

## Why 30 breeding females?

Below ~15–20 lactating cows, fixed costs for chilling, cooling, milking equipment, biosecurity, skilled labour and feed infrastructure are difficult to spread over enough litres. At ~30 adult breeding females, the farm can support a professional routine while remaining small enough to learn before scaling.

The site should be laid out for 50 adult cows from day one, but the expensive equipment should be bought only for Phase 1 throughput.

## Milk-price scenarios

The National Dairy Development Board currently publishes a minimum raw-milk price of **NPR 65/L** for milk with 4 fat and 8 SNF.

The model therefore uses:
- **NPR 65/L:** conservative bulk-sale floor,
- **NPR 72.5/L:** base blended realization assumption,
- **NPR 77.5/L:** stronger direct/local sales mix,
- **NPR 85/L+:** direct-sale upside only; not used as the base investment case.

Do not invest on the assumption that all milk can be sold directly at retail pricing.

## Year-2 steady-state snapshot — 30 adult breeding females

At 30 adult females:
- average lactating cows: **23.1**
- modeled saleable milk: **~414.5 L/day**
- annual saleable milk: **~151,300 L**
- modeled cash OPEX: **~NPR 83.8 lakh/year**
- modeled cash production cost: **~NPR 55.4/L**, before depreciation, owner salary, loan interest and tax.

Milk-only cash surplus:
- at NPR 65/L: **~NPR 14.6 lakh/year**
- at NPR 72.5/L: **~NPR 25.9 lakh/year**
- at NPR 77.5/L: **~NPR 33.5 lakh/year**

These are operating surpluses, not accounting profit.

A full economic cost including depreciation, owner/manager time and financing can push the effective break-even materially above the cash-cost figure. Therefore the business should target either:
1. higher milk yield without sacrificing fertility,
2. lower feed cost through own forage,
3. higher realized milk price,
4. or additional margin from calves/heifers, manure, biogas and value-added dairy.

## Feed model — 30 adult steady state

Illustrative daily ration:

| Group | Silage kg | Green fodder kg | Straw kg | Concentrate kg |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Lactating cow | 18 | 12 | 4 | 6 |
| Dry cow | 12 | 15 | 5 | 2 |
| Heifer | 8 | 12 | 3 | 1.5 |
| Calf | 2 | 3 | 1 | 1.2 |

Planning cost assumptions:
- own silage: NPR 4.5/kg,
- own green fodder: NPR 2.5/kg,
- straw: NPR 12/kg,
- concentrate: NPR 50/kg,
- minerals and small supplements added separately.

At the Year-2 modeled herd composition this produces approximately:
- **607 kg silage/day**
- **543 kg green fodder/day**
- **171 kg straw/day**
- **184 kg concentrate/day**

Feed is modeled at ~NPR 59.1 lakh/year in Year 2, about 70% of cash operating cost. This is intentionally consistent with Nepal dairy-farm research in which feed represented about 71% of total cost.

## Fodder-land requirement

The Chitwan silage supply-chain study reported average fodder-maize productivity of **53.9 t/ha/crop**.

For the Year-2 herd:
- silage fed: ~221 t/year,
- allowing 15% field/storage/feed-out loss: ~260 t fresh crop required,
- with two maize crops/year: theoretical area ~3.6 bigha,
- after weather, rotation and execution buffer: **plan 4.5–5 bigha for silage maize**.

Green fodder requirement is ~198 t/year. Using a deliberately conservative planning yield around 100 t/ha/year for cut-and-carry perennial fodder:
- theoretical area ~2.9 bigha,
- with buffer: **plan 3.5–4 bigha perennial green fodder**.

Add seasonal legumes/oats, nursery, rotation and recovery area.

### Phase-1 feed-land recommendation

**8–10 bigha of reliably irrigated productive fodder land** for the 30-adult-cow system.

For a future 50-adult-cow herd with retained youngstock, plan roughly **15–18 bigha** unless significant roughage is contracted externally.

Feed-land numbers must be revised after actual farm yields are measured for two crop cycles.

## Labour

Nepal's current general minimum remuneration is NPR 19,550/month. The model budgets **NPR 25,000/month per farm worker** before statutory benefits and specialist/manager pay.

Phase 1:
- 4 full-time workers equivalent,
- owner/manager or salaried supervisor,
- veterinarian/AI support on contract,
- seasonal machinery/harvest labour as required.

The design goal is not maximum automation. It is to eliminate the jobs where human inconsistency hurts milk quality, heat stress or hygiene.

## Five-year controlled-growth plan

The purpose is not to keep every female calf forever. Grow the adult herd to the designed capacity, then sell surplus high-quality heifers.

| Year | Adult breeding females avg | Lactating avg | Saleable L/day | Cash cost/L |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 27 | 20.8 | 373 | 54.6 |
| 2 | 30 | 23.1 | 415 | 55.4 |
| 3 | 36 | 27.7 | 497 | 56.1 |
| 4 | 43 | 33.1 | 594 | 54.4 |
| 5 | 50 | 38.5 | 691 | 53.5 |

At Year 5, hold the adult herd around 50 unless a second shed/feed block is deliberately approved. Surplus replacement heifers become a potential revenue stream.

## Herd-growth rule

Retain replacements in this order:
1. daughters of cows with strong fertility + milk + udder + feet/legs + heat tolerance,
2. daughters from selected sexed semen,
3. healthy calves with strong growth,
4. only enough heifers to cover culling plus approved expansion.

Do not retain a calf just because it is female.

## Cooling and heat

For western Terai conditions, cooling is treated as production equipment, not decoration:
- high-volume fans,
- properly timed sprinklers/soakers,
- shade and roof insulation,
- unrestricted clean water,
- feed available during cooler hours,
- sufficient feed-bunk space.

The breeding strategy should optimize lifetime profit under heat, not maximum HF percentage.

## Manure, biogas and vermicompost

Phase 1 should separate the manure stream:
1. fresh manure/slurry collection,
2. anaerobic digestion where economical,
3. digestate solids/liquids separation,
4. stabilization/pre-composting,
5. vermicomposting only after temperature/ammonia conditions are suitable.

Do not count large manure revenue in the base dairy case until a real buyer and product specification exist.

## Investment gates

Do not scale from 30 to 50 adult cows until the farm sustains for at least six months:
- lactating fraction >= 75%,
- saleable milk >= 17 L/lactating cow/day,
- conception and pregnancy KPIs on target,
- mastitis and calf mortality within limits,
- feed inventory >= 90 days,
- realized milk price and payment cycle stable,
- cash cost <= 60 NPR/L,
- no chronic heat-stress bottleneck.

Do not buy a large TMR mixer, automated parlour or processing line simply because Banjara has one. Buy it when throughput produces a defensible payback.

## Key risks

1. Milk price stuck near the NPR 65/L floor.
2. Concentrate cost inflation.
3. Summer heat reducing intake, fertility and milk.
4. Buying high-yield cows with poor adaptation or reproductive history.
5. Failure to build silage before herd arrival.
6. Over-retaining heifers and overcrowding.
7. Weak milk collection/payment reliability.
8. Debt service consuming operating margin.
9. Mastitis and infertility.
10. CAPEX creep from copying large-farm automation too early.
